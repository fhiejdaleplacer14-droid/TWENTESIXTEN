1. Introduction

1.1 Background of the Game

What to include: Explain why the group decided to develop the game. Describe the idea, purpose, inspiration, and intended experience.

Many Filipinos who grew up in the province remember the same childhood: buying ice candy at the sari-sari store with coins from a biscuit tin, playing basketball on the barangay court until the streetlights came on, flag ceremonies, simbang gabi, pisonet computer shops, and a Lola calling everyone home "before dark." The group wanted to make a game about that time, and about the feeling of going back to it as a grown-up.

The idea behind TWENTESIXTEN is a simple wish that every child has: to grow up fast. The game shows how that wish looks from the other side. The player returns to Lola's neighborhood (Purok 3) as an adult. Walking around and talking to the neighbors brings back childhood memories, and each memory is played as a small mini-game. The memories slowly lead the player to a tin box buried under an old tree, and to the real reason everyone in the neighborhood is busy preparing chairs, food, and coffee for the evening.

The intended experience is quiet, warm, and emotional. The game has no enemies, no timer for the whole game, and no game-over screen. The emotional impact comes from the dialogue, the environment, the changing afternoon light, the folk-song music, the small discoveries, and the final reveal. Its closing lines sum up the purpose: "We spent our whole childhood in a hurry to grow up. It was the dumbest thing we ever wished for."

2. Game Overview

2.1 Game Concept

What to include: Explain the game in 1–2 paragraphs as if introducing it to someone who has never played it.

You come home to your Lola's house in a small Filipino barangay for a short visit. It is a sunny afternoon. Lola is outside and asks you to buy vinegar from Ate Cora's sari-sari store, then asks you to look for her old biscuit tin with the dancing ladies on the lid. As you walk around asking the neighbors about it, familiar places bring memories back: the store, the basketball court, the school flagpole, the chapel, the market, the bakery, the plaza fountain, the pisonet, the creek, and Lola's bench. Each memory turns the screen golden, shows the kids you used to play with, and lets you play a short mini-game as your younger self.

Meanwhile the neighbors keep giving you small errands: glue and nails for Mang Tonyo, a message about chairs for Ma'am Linda, a list for Ate Mila's carinderia, bread from Kuya Berto's panaderia. The afternoon turns golden, then to sunset, then to dusk. When you finally dig up the tin box under the old tree and bring it home, you understand why the whole street has been borrowing chairs and making coffee. The game ends quietly on Lola's bench.

2.2 Game Genre

What to include: Identify the genre, such as action, adventure, platformer, racing, puzzle, RPG, strategy, simulation, horror, sports, or educational.

Primary genre: Adventure (story-driven exploration / narrative adventure). The player explores a single connected neighborhood, talks to characters, follows objectives, and uncovers a story. It is close to games often called "walking simulators" or "cozy narrative" games, but with more interaction.

Secondary elements: short arcade / timing mini-games (catching, timing, rhythm, dodging, stealth, hide-and-seek), light simulation (neighbors with their own needs and daily routines, traffic, physics objects), and one dialogue-choice scene.

2.3 Target Players

What to include: Describe the intended age group, users, skill level, and number of players.

  • Age group: Teens and adults (about 13 and up). The game has no violence or mature content. Its themes (growing up, family, loss, and a death in the family) are best understood by older players. Younger players can still play it.
  • Intended users: Filipino players, especially those who grew up in or visited a provincial barangay, and anyone who enjoys short, emotional story games.
  • Skill level: Beginner. Movement and one action button are enough. Mini-games are short and forgiving: losing one never stops the story.
  • Number of players: 1 (single player).

3. Game Objectives

3.1 General Objective

What to include: State the main objective of the game project.

To develop a complete, playable, story-driven 3D web game, set in a Filipino provincial neighborhood around 2016, that lets the player explore, interact with neighbors, and relive childhood memories, and that delivers a meaningful emotional ending about Lola and the wish to grow up too fast. The game should be finishable in about 15 to 20 minutes.

3.2 Specific Objectives

What to include: List 3–5 measurable objectives, such as implementing player controls, enemies, scoring, levels, and interactive environments.

  1. Player controls on three input types: walking and running with keyboard (WASD/arrow keys), gamepad, and an on-screen touch stick, plus one interact button (E / Space / gamepad A / touch button).
  2. An interactive neighborhood: one connected map (92 × 50 tiles) with at least 10 landmark locations, 6 story NPCs, 9 neighbors who move around on their own, animals, traffic, and objects that can be pushed or knocked over.
  3. A data-driven story and quest system: 9 main objectives, 4 side errands, and story flags stored in data files, with automatic saving after every story step and a Continue option.
  4. Ten playable memory mini-games, each with its own rules and a win and lose result that changes the memory's dialogue.
  5. A complete ending sequence (wake scene, Lola's letter, bench scene, film-style end credits), with the full game text available in three languages (English, Tagalog, Bisaya).

4. Game Story / Concept

4.1 Game Story

What to include: Describe the setting, main character, conflict, goal, major events, and ending.

Setting. Purok 3, a small Filipino provincial barangay. Two streets are joined by three alleys. Along them are Lola's house, Mang Tonyo's vulcanizing shop, the basketball court, Ate Cora's sari-sari store, a pisonet, Aling Nena's house, a carinderia, and a panaderia. North of the upper street are the elementary school, the plaza fountain, an empty lot with an old tree, the chapel, and the palengke (market). A dirt path leads south to the creek. The story takes place over one afternoon, from bright day to golden hour, sunset, and dusk.

Main character. The player (Lola's apo, or grandchild). They grew up in this neighborhood and now live in the city ("Manila"), and have come home only "until Sunday." The player is never named. In memories, they appear as their child self.

Conflict. The real conflict is internal. As a child, the player always wanted to be "big" and spent the whole childhood wishing time would go faster. Now grown up, they have drifted away from home and from Lola. Underneath the day's errands, something is clearly wrong: the neighbors act strangely, Cora refuses payment for the vinegar, and everyone is preparing chairs and food "for tonight."

Goal. Find Lola's lost tin box by asking around the neighborhood and following the memories it brings back.

Major events.

  1. Arrival. Lola greets the player ("Have you eaten?") and sends them to buy vinegar at Cora's store.
  2. The tin box. Back home, Lola asks the player to find her old biscuit tin. She tells them to walk slowly and to be home before dark.
  3. Asking around. Cora remembers the tin full of one-peso coins (Memory: the store) and gives the player an old photograph of four kids, taken by someone nobody remembers. Mang Tonyo, Ma'am Linda, and others remember the gang running to the court and the school.
  4. The errand chain. Neighbors pass the player small favors: glue and nails to Tonyo, a message to Linda about borrowing the school's chairs, a list for Mila's carinderia (pancit, puto, "lots of coffee"), and bread from Berto. Each one quietly hints at a gathering that night.
  5. Memories return. Visiting old places plays memories as mini-games. After at least six memories and the errands, the player looks at the photo again and remembers a tree. The light turns golden.
  6. The tree. Under the old tree the player digs up the rusty tin: a marble, three teks cards, and a letter they wrote as a child to their future self ("Buy Lola a new tin. I took this one. Sorry Lola."). The sun begins to set.
  7. The wake. Walking home at dusk, the player finds every neighbor's chairs set out in front of Lola's house under a tent and string lights. Cora: "We've been looking for you all afternoon." Tonyo: "Our condolences, anak." Lola has passed away.

Ending. Inside, Lola looks like she is resting her eyes after lunch. Taped inside the tin's lid is a second letter in Lola's handwriting: she found the buried letter as well, read it, put it back, and asks only, "Just come home once in a while. Even if it's not before dark." The player sits on the worn bench outside. For a moment Lola appears faintly beside them and whispers, "Have you eaten?", then fades. The closing lines appear, the title comes up, and the credits roll over the team's own footage of kites at dusk. The afternoon with Lola, the dream-like memories, and the bench all come together into the final realization: the player cannot go back to the past they were in such a hurry to leave.

5. Game Features

5.1 Major Features

What to include: List and explain the major implemented features.

  1. 3D voxel neighborhood with pixel-art characters. The whole barangay is built in 3D from code (houses with tin roofs, a jeepney, tricycles, the court, the fountain, trees) and lit in real time with shadows. Characters stay as crisp 2D pixel sprites that always face the camera.
  2. Changing time of day. The light moves through four phases tied to the story: afternoon, golden hour, sunset, and dusk. Street lamps, lit windows, and glow increase as it gets dark.
  3. Story and quest system. Objectives, side errands, and story flags are driven by a data file. Each NPC answers differently depending on how far the story has gone.
  4. Ten memory mini-games. Each memory is a short cinematic (golden flash, camera zoom, ghost kids) with a mini-game played as the younger self.
  5. A living neighborhood. Nine neighbors decide for themselves where to go based on needs (hunger, errands, rest, company, fun, quiet). They walk along the sidewalks, chat with each other, and head home at dusk.
  6. Traffic and physics. Jeepneys and tricycles drive down the streets, brake and honk, and knock the player down the street if they step in front. Pails, boxes, and a ball can be pushed, kicked, and knocked over. A neighbor comes running to help after an accident.
  7. Items you can look at. The photograph (Q) and the letter (R) can be opened at any time. The photograph's description changes once the player has found the letter, revealing a new detail.
  8. Three languages. All text, including shop signs in the 3D world, is available in English, Tagalog, and Bisaya.
  9. Music and sound made in the browser. Filipino folk songs and all sound effects are synthesized live with the Web Audio API; no recorded audio files are used.
  10. Automatic save and Continue. Progress is saved after every story step.
  11. Play anywhere. Keyboard, gamepad, and touch controls; installable and playable offline (PWA).
  12. Cinematic ending and end credits over the team's own kite footage, with the team's photo stickers.

6.2 Gameplay Rules

What to include: Explain the rules, objectives, winning conditions, losing conditions, and player limitations.

  • Objective: Follow the objective shown at the top of the screen, from "Lola is outside. Talk to her." to "Go inside."
  • Interacting: When the player stands within reach of a person or object (about two tiles), an "E" prompt appears above it. Pressing E starts the interaction. NPCs say different things depending on story progress.
  • Memories: Memories at old places unlock only after Lola asks about the tin box. Each memory can be played once. Its mini-game can be won or lost; both results count as a found memory, and only the dialogue changes.
  • Progression rule: To reach the old tree, the player must have (a) the store, court, and school memories, (b) finished the errand chain up to Kuya Berto, and (c) found at least 6 of the 10 memories.
  • Winning condition: Dig up the tin box at the old tree, walk home, and enter Lola's door. This plays the ending. Finding all 10 memories adds an extra line to the ending.
  • Losing condition: There is no game over. The game is designed so the story can always be finished. Being hit by a jeepney or tricycle only knocks the player down the street for a moment (the game counts the hits and shows the total in the end credits).
  • Player limitations: The player cannot walk through buildings, fences, trees, parked vehicles, or stopped jeepneys, and cannot leave the map. Movement is locked during dialogue, memories, and the ending. Story rules play one at a time.

6.3 Scoring System

What to include: Explain how points, lives, health, rewards, penalties, or other game statistics are calculated.

TWENTESIXTEN does not use a points score or a health bar, because the goal is the story rather than a high score. Progress and results are tracked as follows:

  • Memories found: shown on the HUD as "memories n / 10." This is the main progress statistic.
  • Mini-game result: each mini-game has its own counter (coins, shots, notes, lives, time). A win sets a won_<memory> flag and plays a "won" line; a loss plays a "lost" line. Neither result blocks progress.
  • Times run over: each jeepney/tricycle hit adds 1 to a counter that is saved and shown in the end credits.
  • Rewards: story items (the photograph, the letter), new objectives and errands, and changes to the light and music.

7.3 Game Environment

What to include: Describe maps, stages, worlds, backgrounds, lighting, terrain, and other environmental elements.

Map. One map of 92 × 50 tiles (each tile is 32 game pixels). It has two east–west streets (each with sidewalks and a 4-tile road), three connecting alleys, and a dirt path down to the creek.

  • Buildings and terrain: houses and shops built from 3D tiles with corrugated tin roofs, awnings, and lit windows; grass, sidewalks with curbs, lower asphalt roads, dirt paths, the creek, and a tree line along the top edge.
  • Filipino details: shop signs (SARI-SARI, PISONET, CARINDERIA, PANADERIA, PALENGKE, TODA), the "WELCOME TO PUROK 3" arch, "BAWAL UMIHI DITO" and "BAWAL TUMAMBAY" signs, a jeepney with stripes, tricycles waiting for fares, motorbikes on the sidewalk, potted plants by every door, snacks clipped over the store window, a fishball cart, banderitas, and power lines.
  • Lighting: four lighting setups (afternoon, golden, sunset, dusk), each with its own sun direction and color, sky gradient, fog color, street lamp brightness, window glow, and bloom. The game blends smoothly from one to the next. Memories briefly warm the whole scene to gold.
  • Moving life: neighbors, a kid shooting hoops, a dog, chickens, butterflies, birds overhead, smoke from the food stalls, leaves falling from the old tree, and traffic with headlights at night.
  • Backgrounds: the title screen shows the same 3D street at sunset with the camera slowly drifting along the main street.

8. User Interface Design

8.1 Main Menu

What to include: Insert a screenshot or design and explain its functions.

The main menu shows the title "TWENTESIXTEN" in a serif font over a darkened 3D view of the street at sunset. The menu items fade in one by one on the left:

  • Start (first play) or Continue and New game (when a save exists). Continue shows the saved objective next to it.
  • Controls: a side panel listing keyboard, gamepad, and touch controls.
  • Settings: volume (0–10), text speed (slow / normal / fast), effects (low / medium / high), language, and fullscreen.
  • Credits: art, font, engine, sound, and music credits.

On the very first visit, the game asks for a language before anything else ("Language · Wika · Pinulongan"), suggesting one based on the browser language. The menu works with keyboard, mouse, gamepad, and touch. A hint line at the bottom shows the controls for the current panel.

8.2 Gameplay Interface / HUD

What to include: Document health, score, timer, inventory, controls, minimap, or other interface elements.

  • Objective (top): the current main goal, e.g., "Ask around about Lola's tin box."
  • Errand: the current side favor, e.g., "errand: Bring the glue and nails to Mang Tonyo."
  • Memories counter: "memories n / 10".
  • Inventory hints: "Q photograph" and "R letter" appear once the player has those items.
  • Interaction prompt: a small floating "E" key over whatever the player can talk to or look at.
  • Controls help: a one-line hint at the start ("WASD / arrows to walk, Shift to run, E to talk and look"), hidden after the first interaction.
  • Dialogue box: typewriter text with the speaker's name plate and pixel portrait. Narration has no name. Memory lines appear without a box.
  • Cinematic bars: black bars at the top and bottom during memories and the ending.
  • Mini-game banner and status: instructions at the top (e.g., "Press E when the marker is in the gold") and a live status (coins, shots, notes, lives, seconds).
  • Touch controls (phones/tablets): a floating thumbstick on the left, E / photo / letter buttons on the right; hidden until the first touch.
  • No health bar, score, timer, or minimap, by design.

How it fits together. The game logic runs in Phaser using the 2D map's pixel coordinates (32 pixels per tile). Phaser's own camera for the gameplay scene is hidden. 12. Game Implementation

12.1 Player Implementation

What to include: Explain movement, jumping, attack, health, animation, collision, and related implementation.

  • Movement: the player is a Phaser Arcade Physics sprite. Walking speed is 120 px/s; running (Shift / RB) is 200 px/s. Keyboard input is normalized so diagonals are not faster. Sticks (touch or gamepad) give analog speed, at least 45% when slightly pushed, with a small dead zone.
  • Direction and animation: four directions (up, down, left, right), each with a 3-frame walk (stand + 2 steps). On a diagonal, the sideways frames are used. Running plays the animation 1.7× faster. Footstep sounds play twice per walk cycle.
  • Interaction: every frame, the nearest interactable within 58 px is found and an "E" prompt floats over it. Pressing E turns the player toward the target and starts its story rule.
  • Getting hit by traffic: the player is thrown along the street in a simple projectile arc (gravity 900 px/s²), spins, bounces once, lies dazed with an "@_@" emote, then walks back to the sidewalk. A neighbor runs over to check on them.
  • No jumping, attack, or health: these were not part of the design.

12.2 Enemy Implementation

What to include: Explain enemy behavior, movement, AI, attacks, health, and defeat.

TWENTESIXTEN has no enemies; it is a story game about coming home. The characters with AI and the things the player must avoid are:

  • Neighbors (needs-based AI, Life.js): each of the 9 neighbors has needs (hunger, errand, rest, social, fun, calm) that grow over time at their own rates (set in residents.json). When free, a neighbor scores every place in the barangay against their strongest need, walks there along a network of sidewalk corners (path-finding over a graph of nodes and edges), stays a while, chats with anyone nearby, then decides again. As the light fades, "home" wins. Their dialogue depends on where they are going or what they are doing.
  • Story NPCs: stay near their spot but take a few steps and look around; they stop and turn to face the player during conversations.
  • Animals: chickens scatter from whoever approaches; the dog chases chickens and the ball; birds and butterflies drift.
  • Traffic (hazard, Traffic.js): jeepneys and tricycles drive along fixed lanes, one per lane at a time. They brake for anyone in the road (25% of drivers are "reckless" and only honk), raise dust, send loose objects flying, and knock the player down the street on contact. They have solid footprints when stopped and headlights at dusk.
  • Mini-game opponents: Aling Ising in Creek Sneak looks up at random times with an 8-tile, about 64° view cone, and the falling blocks in the Pisonet game.

12.3 Collision Detection

What to include: Explain player/environment, player/enemy, player/item, and other important collisions.

  • Player / environment: Arcade Physics collider between the player and a static group of solid rectangles (buildings, fences, walls, trees, stalls, parked vehicles), built from the map data in neighborhood.js. The player's body is only the feet (10 × 5 px), so the head can overlap walls and it looks natural in the top-down view. World bounds keep the player inside the 92 × 50-tile map.
  • Player / traffic: each vehicle checks whether the player's feet are inside its lane area in front of it; if it cannot stop, the player is knocked (see 12.1). Stopped vehicles add a solid footprint.
  • Player / objects (Toys.js): pails, boxes, and a ball have footprints, weights, and friction. Walking into a loose object pushes it; running into a pail knocks it over (E stands it back up). The ball is kicked harder when running and bounces off solids. Chairs and the trash bin (basurahan) are fixed.
  • Player / interactables: distance check (58 px) to find the nearest person or object to talk to.
  • Story zones: rectangles that start a story rule when the player walks in (e.g., arriving home after finding the tin).
  • Mini-games: distance and range checks (coins picked up, bread in the bag, coin landing in the basin, blocks hitting the ship, Ising's view cone vs. cover).

12.4 Game State and Progression

What to include: Explain score, health, lives, level progression, game over, victory, saving/loading, and other state management.

Story system (data-driven). All story state is a set of named flags (e.g., errand, suka, asked, m_store, bread_done, ready, tin, arrived). In story.json, each interactable has an ordered list of rules; the first rule whose conditions hold is played. A rule's steps can show dialogue, play a memory, or open a letter; when it finishes it can set flags, give an item, change the light phase, set the objective or errand, trigger an event, or start the ending. "Derived" rules fire by themselves when their conditions are met (for example, the realization that unlocks the tree).

// src/data/story.json (excerpt)
"tree": [
  { "if": ["tin"], "steps": [{ "say": "tree_after" }] },
  { "if": ["ready"],
    "steps": [{ "say": "tree_dig" }, { "memory": "tree" }, { "say": "tree_found" }],
    "set": ["tin"], "give": "letter", "phase": "sunset",
    "event": "setWake", "objective": "bring_tin" },
  { "steps": [{ "say": "tree" }] }
]
  • Progression: objectives move through 9 stages (talk_lola → buy_vinegar → bring_vinegar → ask_tin → old_places → more → tree → bring_tin → go_inside). The light moves through afternoon → golden → sunset → dusk.
  • Victory: entering Lola's door after arriving home triggers the ending; if all 10 memories were found, an extra line plays. The save is then cleared.
  • Game over / lives / health: not used (see 6.2).
  • Saving and loading: after every story step, the game writes one save to localStorage: flags, items, objective, errand, memories found, light phase, hit count, and player position (with a version number). "Continue" restores all of it and re-checks any derived rule that was about to fire. If storage is blocked, the game still plays.
  • Settings (volume, text speed, effects, language) are saved separately.

15. Problems Encountered and Solutions

The problems below are the ones whose solutions can be seen in the final code. The team should add any other problems they remember from development.

15.1 Development Problems

What to include: Document important technical, design, asset, performance, or programming problems encountered.

  1. Showing a 3D world while keeping simple 2D gameplay code. Writing all movement, collision, and NPC logic directly in 3D would be much harder.
  2. Performance on weaker devices and phones. Real-time shadows and glow are expensive, and the town is made of hundreds of separate pieces, each one a draw call.
  3. Browsers block audio until the player interacts. Music and sound could not start on page load.
  4. Music licensing. Popular songs from 2016 would fit the mood but cannot be published without permission.
  5. Losing progress when the browser tab is closed, and errors when localStorage is blocked (private browsing).
  6. Supporting three languages without duplicating story logic, including signs drawn inside the 3D world.
  7. Making the neighborhood feel alive instead of empty, with NPCs that do not just stand still.
  8. Playing on phones without a keyboard.
  9. Large ending video slowing down offline caching.
  10. Story pacing: the player must not reach the ending before the important memories and clues.

holds(rule) {
  const all = (rule.if || []).every((f) =>
    f[0] === '!' ? !this.flags.has(f.slice(1)) : this.flags.has(f));
  const any = !rule.anyOf || rule.anyOf.some((f) => this.flags.has(f));
  return all && any && (!rule.count || this.found() >= rule.count);
}

interact(it) {
  if (this.busy) return;
  const rules = it.talk ? [{ steps: [{ lines: it.talk() }] }]
                        : story.rules[it.id] || [{ steps: [{ say: it.id }] }];
  const rule = rules.find((r) => this.holds(r));
  if (rule) this.play(rule, it);
}
